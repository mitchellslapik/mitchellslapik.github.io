<p>
I am an MD/PhD candidate at <a href="https://gsbs.uth.edu/mdphd/">McGovern Medical School</a> and a systems and computational neuroscientist studying how patterns of neural activity shape behavior. I want to bring these approaches to psychiatric illness.
</p>

<p>
During my PhD in Valentin Dragoi's laboratory, I used large-scale electrophysiology in nonhuman primates to study how behavioral state shapes neural representations and communication between visual and prefrontal cortex. I am co-first author of a study accepted in principle at <em>Nature Neuroscience</em> showing that locomotion enhances this communication and improves visual detection. I have also studied how prefrontal neurons represent the body and used computational models and human 7-T fMRI to compare biological and artificial visual representations. My research has appeared in <em>Neural Computation</em> and the <em>Journal of Vision</em>, with support from <a href="https://researchtraining.nih.gov/programs/fellowships/F30">NIH F30</a> and <a href="https://www.uth.edu/ccts/education-training/tl1-program">TL1</a> fellowships.
</p>

<p>
Before medicine, I studied philosophy in the Honors Program at <a href="https://www.swarthmore.edu/">Swarthmore College</a> and the neuropsychiatric consequences of cerebellar disease at <a href="https://www.jhu.edu/">Johns Hopkins University</a>.
</p>

<p>
In psychiatry, I am interested in how illness unfolds over time. Patients with similar symptoms can follow different courses and respond differently to treatment. A clinical score tells us where someone is, but not how they got there. My current research uses longitudinal cohorts to study recovery and subsequent vulnerability. My goal is to understand what changes before relapse, what distinguishes recovery, and why the same treatment helps one patient but not another.
</p>
